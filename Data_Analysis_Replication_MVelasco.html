Introduction

This assignment explores the application of R as a tool to reproduce the quantitative analysis of published research. In this case, I brought one of the most interesting proposals in Amazonian Soil Sciences. During centuries the Amazon was considered as a “counterfeit paradise”, a “green hell” or a ”natural wild and pristine forest. Current research, especially Brazilian Archaeologists and Anthropologists have questioned those assumptions and have debated the apparent scarcity and environmental limitation of the Amazon through the Terra Preta de Indio or Amazonian Dark Earth. This concept explains the enrichment and fertility of soils in the Amazon basin, in places related with pre-Columbian settlements. The ADE has been an important topic to discuss sustainability and forest management in the Amazon. However,”Silva et. al. (2021) presents an statement about an alternative origin of these soils, arguing an apparent alluvial origin. His claim is supported by geochemical evidence from one location in Central Amazon, showing interesting datasets that explore: chemical composition, statistical projections, quantitative analysis and correlations. As an Anthropological Archaeologist involved in Amazonian Research, I read this article critically and found pertinent explore its data management and methodology to understand how current scholarship is constructing new arguments to foster or question interpretations.

In that regard, this work looks to analyze and reproduce the plots and statistical analysis of Silva et. al. (2021) using R as the resource. This replication data analysis will be composed for chunks divided in sections and following a relation with the article and available data. Finally, I present a section of conclusions that summarize this exercise and show my own perspectives of applied data analysis in Archaeological research. I can conclude that R is a great tool for data analysis but it requires of sustained practice and review of packages and statistical analysis, the need to tidy our data, the necesity to understand our data and the use of plots and visual tools to fulfill that aim, and most important, the limitations that our knowledge and data have in the research practice.

Abstract

Amazonian Dark Earths (ADEs) are unusually fertile soils characterised by elevated concentrations of microscopic charcoal particles, which confer their distinctive colouration. Frequent occurrences of pre-Columbian artefacts at ADE sites led to their ubiquitous classification as Anthrosols (soils of anthropic origin). However, it remains unclear how indigenous peoples created areas of high fertility in one of the most nutrient-impoverished environments on Earth. Here, we report new data from a well-studied ADE site in the Brazilian Amazon, which compel us to reconsider its anthropic origin. The amounts of phosphorus and calcium—two of the least abundant macronutrients in the region—are orders of magnitude higher in ADE profiles than in the surrounding soil. The elevated levels of phosphorus and calcium, which are often interpreted as evidence of human activity at other sites, correlate spatially with trace elements that indicate exogenous mineral sources rather than in situ deposition. Stable isotope ratios of neodymium, strontium, and radiocarbon activity of microcharcoal particles also indicate exogenous inputs from alluvial deposition of carbon and mineral elements to ADE profiles, beginning several thousands of years before the earliest evidence of soil management for plant cultivation in the region. Our data suggest that indigenous peoples harnessed natural processes of landscape formation, which led to the unique properties of ADEs, but were not responsible for their genesis. If corroborated elsewhere, this hypothesis would transform our understanding of human influence in Amazonia, opening new frontiers for the sustainable use of tropical landscapes going forward.

krig_map <- function(df_depth, element_col, depth_val, title_str) {
  df_sub <- df_depth %>%
    filter(`Depth (cm)` == depth_val) %>%
    transmute(x = `Coordinate X`, y = `Coordinate Y`,
              z = as.numeric(.data[[element_col]])) %>%
    filter(!is.na(z))
 
  sp_df <- df_sub
  coordinates(sp_df) <- ~x + y
 
  v <- variogram(z ~ 1, sp_df)
  v_fit <- tryCatch(fit.variogram(v, model = vgm(var(df_sub$z),"Sph", max(v$dist)/2,nugget = var(df_sub$z)*0.1), fit.method = 6),
                    error = function(e) fit.variogram(v, model = vgm(var(df_sub$z), "Exp", max(v$dist)/2, nugget = var(df_sub$z)*0.1), fit.method = 6))
  
  v_fit$range[v_fit$range<0] <- max(v$dist)/2
  v_fit$psill[v_fit$psill<0] <- var(df_sub$z)
 
  x_r <- range(df_sub$x); y_r <- range(df_sub$y)
  grd <- expand.grid(x = seq(x_r[1], x_r[2], length.out = 60),
                     y = seq(y_r[1], y_r[2], length.out = 60))
  coordinates(grd) <- ~x + y; gridded(grd) <- TRUE
 
  k <- krige(z ~ 1, sp_df, grd, model = v_fit, debug.level = 0)
  k_df <- as.data.frame(k)
 
  ggplot(k_df, aes(x = x, y = y, fill = var1.pred)) +
    geom_raster(interpolate = TRUE) +
    geom_point(data = df_sub, aes(x = x, y = y), inherit.aes = FALSE,
               colour = "white", size = 1, alpha = 0.7) +
    scale_fill_gradientn(
      colours = c("#2166ac","#74add1","#ffffbf","#f46d43","#a50026"),
      name    = element_col
    ) +
    labs(title = paste0(depth_val, " cm"), x = NULL, y = NULL) +
    coord_fixed() +
    theme_bw(base_size = 8) +
    theme(plot.title = element_text(size = 8, hjust = 0.5),
          legend.key.height = unit(0.3, "cm"),
          axis.text = element_text(size = 6))
}

Conclusions

This article examines one of the most important debates in Amazonian Archaeological research, the Terra Preta, with a examination of one site with intensive geochemical analysis and radiocarbon dating. The purpose of Silva,et. al. (2021) was to present an alternative explanation to the origin of Terra Preta as product of alluvial deposition instead of a product of indigenous inventiveness millenia before the European conquest.

The manage of data presents interesting incorporation of analysis to construct an argument in favor of his proposal: a not-anthropogenic explanation to the ADE (Amazonian Dark Earths). The geochemical characterization explains the enrichment of the ADE in P, Ca, Sr, Nd, and other elements that the presence of C compose the stock of Terra Preta. However, his research is limited to one site and the authors are calling to the debate of a sustained research tradition in ADE for the last decades.

Between the conclusions based on the data analysis replication, I would like to precise in following quotes:

  1. Mapping with R (See Figure 2 in 04. Reference Profiles)

    Silva, et. al, presents a map in which the measures the concentrations of P and Ca by the depth. In that matter, our excercise permitted to create maps based on the quantitative results of the file 2 or dataset d2. However, the method failed in accuracy due to the points were related to georreferenced coordinates that were not delimited by polygon such as shown in Silva, et. al. (2021). In that order the chart shows non delimited buffers that are not reflecting the same visualization than the article. A solution to this problem could be the combination of GIS tools + R geolibraries. In this case, I decided not to go deeper in this mapping exercise and concentrate in the quantitative graphs.

  2. Math and graphs in a versatil way

    The article was published in Nature Communications. This journal has a high audience and a high rate of citations. However, their articles are brief and concrete due to their publication norms. In that order, the use of data visualization could help articles to get published or rejected. In this case the use of linear scatterplots with error rates and statistical references strengthen the publication. In the case of this replication, R permitted me to reproduce all the charts. The visualization is optimal and present high quality to publications. However, there is a great efffort in the connection of datasets (three in this article) and its analysis in statistical charts and graphic visualizations. It is noticeable that the incorporation of statistical predictions such ANOVA permitted him to estimate hypotheses to argue an alternative origin of ADE. In the precise case of fishing consumption, his calculation according to estimated rates of P in fresh and dry fish, its quantity and the time needed to generate ADE as a cultural processes is mathematically correct, but limited to their data. Possibily, Silva et. al (2021) data could have a different error margin and SE if compared with other datasets using the same tools (t-statistics, ANOVA, etc. )

  3. Tidy data

    One of the problems I found was the representation of datasets in a numeric way. In the Supplementary data, Silva, et. al. (2021) present a table with correlations between 28 chemical elements. However, the table’s font size is too small to have a good visualization. Possibility, the table shows a necessary piece of data but it is visually non-efficient. When I deployed the data in R it was unmanageable (see Supplementary Table 1: Correlation Heatmap in chunk 18). My current knowledge in coding did not allow me to reproduce this table, however, the data was considered to reproduce the other graphic products and some statistical exercises. The dataset 3 presented a conflict between two columns and it demanded to edit it to solve a possible table plot. In that case, the attempts to tidy the data were not fruitful, but it is a great excercise to comprehend the importance of building understandable, comprehensible and tidy data sets for our analysis.

  4. Data Analysis in R

    R is a powerful analytical tool, it permits to manage great datasets and explore them in a dynamic way. I found that the construction of ordered lines is the secret to accurate analysis. The exploration of packages and their respective tools demand of a constant update. In that case, my interest is to keep practicing and exploring data analysis replication to understand the data management as a crucial component of scientific work. My focus, is to review how the current scholarship is working their data and presentation to replicate analysis and discuss my own research in the most appropriate way. Finally, ADE is my research interest and most of its scholarship is based in quantitative analysis, I found R and statistical analysis in R, gitHub and other resources the start of a shift in my own academic pathway and a great incorporation to Anthropological, Geographical and Archaeological research.
